## Decoding Sperm Morphology: What Teratozoospermia Means for Conception and IVF

As you navigate your fertility journey (TTC), you might encounter terms like "teratozoospermia" and feel overwhelmed or anxious. While abnormal sperm morphology can be a factor in male infertility, a diagnosis doesn't necessarily close the door to natural conception and holds specific implications for assisted reproductive technologies (ART) like In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) and Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI).

In this article, as an OB/GYN and fertility specialist, I'll provide an objective and calm explanation, from evaluating sperm morphology to understanding its impact on pregnancy and the modern reproductive medicine approaches available. While debates on sperm morphology and fertility continue, I hope this information helps you understand what we currently know and how to navigate this aspect of your TTC journey.

### What is Sperm Morphology?

Sperm morphology assessment is a crucial part of a semen analysis. It involves examining the shape and size of the sperm's head, midpiece, and tail, evaluating the percentage of normally shaped sperm based on criteria like those from the World Health Organization (WHO) or the more stringent Kruger classification. Sperm with normal morphology are generally thought to function more efficiently in fertilization.

★**The standing of this test is lower than most people assume.** Let me put the conclusion first.

A 2019 review states that **"current evidence suggests sperm morphology has low predictive value for pregnancy success, for both natural and assisted reproduction"** (PMID: 31203470). The authors conclude that morphology "seems to play less of a role than initially thought."

A 2017 review goes further (PMID: 28692759):

*   ★**For diagnosing infertility, sperm morphology assessment has very poor sensitivity and specificity**
*   ★**Scoring the percentage of features such as thin head, amorphous head, or bent neck is of little clinical use** — most are physiological traits
*   ★**There is very little evidence that multiple-defect indices (SDI, TZI, MAI) are relevant**
*   ★**The test shows a major lack of analytical reliability** — results shift depending on who reads the slide

The authors conclude that "questions are raised on the real clinical impact of this test."

The exception, where clinical meaning is clear, is a set of **specific defects affecting essentially every sperm** (globozoospermia, macrocephaly, decapitated sperm syndrome, fibrous sheath dysplasia). These are often linked to genetic disorders and are a different matter from a "X% normal forms" score.

★Both of these are **reviews**, not comparative trials. They set out how the test is regarded.

### Diagnosing Teratozoospermia and Its Implications

Teratozoospermia refers to a condition where a low percentage of sperm in an ejaculate have normal morphology. For instance, current WHO guidelines may define teratozoospermia as having less than 4% normal-shaped sperm. Even with this diagnosis, it's rare for all sperm to be abnormal; a certain number of normal-shaped sperm are usually still present.

Teratozoospermia often presents without any noticeable symptoms. Therefore, it's commonly diagnosed for the first time during a semen analysis when a couple is trying to conceive.

### Impact on Natural Conception: The Ongoing Debate

A diagnosis of teratozoospermia does not automatically mean that natural conception is impossible. While studies suggest that abnormal sperm morphology may affect fertilization capacity and embryo development, other factors like sperm count and motility must also be considered holistically.

As above, **the predictive value of sperm morphology is low for natural conception too** (PMID: 31203470). No threshold has been established below which natural conception becomes unlikely. It's crucial to discuss your specific situation with a [fertility specialist to understand your options.](/) And please, honor your feelings during this time.

### Teratozoospermia in In Vitro Fertilization (IVF) and Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI)

In the realm of Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART), teratozoospermia is evaluated from a different perspective.

#### In Vitro Fertilization (IVF)

In conventional IVF, multiple sperm are placed around an egg to encourage natural fertilization. In cases of teratozoospermia, the sperm's ability to penetrate the egg's outer layer (zona pellucida) or activate the egg might be compromised, potentially leading to lower fertilization rates. However, if sperm concentration and motility are good, successful fertilization can still occur with IVF.

#### Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection (ICSI)

ICSI is a technique where a single sperm is directly injected into an egg. This method becomes a highly effective option for severe teratozoospermia or when fertilization has failed during conventional IVF. With ICSI, the embryologist selects the sperm that appears most morphologically normal, thereby potentially overcoming the direct impact of morphology on fertilization. It's important to note that even with ICSI, other sperm quality factors (beyond morphology) can still influence embryo quality and pregnancy rates.

### Navigating Teratozoospermia: Lifestyle and Treatment Options

If you've been diagnosed with teratozoospermia, identifying any underlying causes is important. Conditions like varicocele or hormonal imbalances, if treatable, might lead to improvements in sperm morphology.

Lifestyle modifications are often recommended, but their direct impact on sperm morphology should be viewed with caution. Practices beneficial for overall health, such as quitting smoking, reducing alcohol intake, maintaining a balanced diet, engaging in moderate exercise, and managing stress, may positively influence spermatogenesis. However, strong scientific evidence definitively showing dramatic improvements in morphology from these changes alone is not yet established. Similarly, while some supplements support general health, their direct effect on sperm morphology is an area for further research. Prioritizing your overall well-being is always a positive step. For more tailored guidance, [consider consulting with a fertility professional.](/)

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1: Can diet improve sperm morphology?**

A1: A balanced diet is vital for overall health and may positively influence sperm production. However, there's no strong, conclusive evidence yet that specific foods or supplements can "guarantee" or dramatically improve sperm morphology. It's best to consider dietary changes as part of general health maintenance rather than a direct cure for morphological issues. Your effort to prioritize health is commendable.

**Q2: If diagnosed with teratozoospermia, should we give up on natural conception?**

A2: Not necessarily. The possibility of natural conception varies depending on the severity of teratozoospermia and other semen analysis parameters (concentration, motility). Also, sperm morphology assessment has subjective elements. It's essential not to let your anxieties overwhelm you. Instead, talk openly with a fertility specialist to discuss your specific situation and explore the best options for your TTC journey. Your feelings of uncertainty are completely understandable.

**Q3: Are there additional tests beyond semen analysis that should be considered?**

A3: **The sequence is not "poor morphology, therefore genetic testing."** According to a review of genetic testing in infertility (Krausz C, et al. Expert Rev Mol Diagn. 2018. PMID: 29540081), the **indication for karyotype and Y-chromosome microdeletion testing is based on sperm count** — severe oligozoospermia or azoospermia. Morphology triggers gene testing only for the **specific phenotypes** named earlier in this article, such as globozoospermia and macrocephalic sperm.

The same review notes that known genetic factors account for **20–25%** of male factor infertility, and that in about **40% of cases the cause remains unknown**. Note that this is **a review article, not a comparative study reporting test accuracy or new data.** On sperm DNA fragmentation testing specifically, [this article](/en/blog/sperm-dna-fragmentation-infertility-hidden-cause) covers who actually needs it. Which tests are appropriate for your circumstances is a conversation with your doctor.

## Conclusion

Teratozoospermia refers to a condition of abnormal sperm morphology, but its diagnosis does not immediately equate to infertility. While its impact on natural conception remains a subject of ongoing debate, advanced reproductive technologies like IVF and ICSI offer significant possibilities for achieving pregnancy.

I hope this information provides clarity and support to couples facing a teratozoospermia diagnosis or those with general fertility concerns. Remember, you don't have to navigate this alone. Open communication with a trusted fertility specialist is key to finding the best path forward for you.
