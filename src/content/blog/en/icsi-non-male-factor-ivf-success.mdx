## ICSI for Non-Male-Factor Infertility: Does It Really Improve IVF Success?

If you are going through fertility treatment, you may have heard the term "ICSI" — intracytoplasmic sperm injection. ICSI is widely known as an effective treatment for male-factor infertility, where there is a problem with the sperm. But when infertility has a cause other than male factor — unexplained infertility, or ovulatory factor infertility, for example — the question of whether ICSI truly raises IVF success rates compared with conventional IVF has been asked again and again. In this article, I want to address that important question objectively, based on the current evidence. Treatment decisions should be made through full discussion with your specialist, with your own feelings respected throughout.

### What Is ICSI (Intracytoplasmic Sperm Injection)?

ICSI is an advanced assisted reproductive technique in which a single selected sperm is injected directly into the egg to achieve fertilization. It is used mainly when there is a male-factor cause such as low sperm count, low motility, or abnormal morphology. This can increase the chance of achieving fertilization even in cases where conventional IVF would struggle.

### How It Differs From Conventional IVF

In conventional IVF, retrieved eggs and sperm are placed together in the same culture medium and fertilization is allowed to happen naturally. With this approach, sperm must pass through the outer layer of the egg under their own power to achieve fertilization. The major difference with ICSI is that this step is assisted artificially. Which method to choose is considered carefully, based on the cause of infertility and the individual situation.

### How Effective Is ICSI in Non-Male-Factor Infertility?

So, is ICSI effective for infertility with causes other than male factor — problems with egg quality or ovulation, or unexplained infertility?

A 2020 meta-analysis pooled 20 randomised controlled trials (PMID: 32018045). The results were as follows.

| Outcome | Result |
|---|---|
| **Live birth rate** | No difference (RR 1.08, 95% CI 0.79–1.49, p = 0.62) |
| Fertilisation rate | No difference (RR 0.94, 95% CI 0.82–1.07, p = 0.34) |
| Implantation rate | No difference (RR 1.25, 95% CI 0.92–1.68, p = 0.15) |
| **Clinical pregnancy rate** | **Significantly higher with conventional IVF** (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.11–1.49, p = 0.001) |

So it is not merely that ICSI cannot be said to be better — **for clinical pregnancy rate, conventional IVF came out ahead.**

### But conventional IVF carries a different risk

In the same paper, the authors raise a second point: **conventional IVF carries a higher risk of total fertilisation failure.**

Total fertilisation failure means that none of the retrieved eggs fertilise. When that happens the cycle cannot proceed to transfer, and the burden and cost of the retrieval are lost entirely. Because ICSI injects the sperm directly, it makes that outcome easier to avoid.

This is what makes the decision genuinely difficult. **On average, conventional IVF performs better. But if what you most want to avoid is the rare outcome of zero, ICSI has the advantage.** Which of those weighs more depends on how many eggs were retrieved and on what has happened in previous cycles.

Where there is no male factor, egg and sperm are capable of fertilising on their own in the culture medium. The point is that adding the intervention of ICSI does not push the result higher.

This suggests that when male factor is absent, eggs and sperm have sufficient capacity to fertilize naturally in the culture medium, so the additional intervention of ICSI is not necessarily required.

### Limitations and Points to Consider

ICSI is a sophisticated technique, and there are several things to weigh before using it.

*   **Higher cost**: ICSI tends to be more expensive than conventional IVF. The burden of treatment costs is an important issue for people undergoing fertility treatment, and your own financial situation needs to be part of the picture.
*   **Invasiveness**: Because a needle is inserted into the egg to deliver the sperm, it is more invasive to the egg than conventional IVF. There is therefore a small possibility of damage to the egg.
*   **Emotional burden**: A more complex treatment process can also add to the emotional load.

These factors mean that where ICSI does not bring a clear benefit for non-male-factor infertility, it can become an unnecessary burden. When choosing treatment, it is important to discuss these points fully with your specialist and to decide while honoring how you feel. You may well feel uneasy about treatment decisions. No emotion here is unnatural. Your feelings deserve respect.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1: What should I do if ICSI is recommended even though there is no male factor?

A1: Start by asking your doctor to explain the reasoning in detail. Depending on the individual situation — a risk of fertilization failure, or a low fertilization rate in a previous IVF cycle, for example — ICSI may indeed be recommended. If you have questions or concerns, it is important to keep asking until the explanation makes sense to you. Considering a second opinion is also a legitimate option.

### Q2: Are there genetic risks associated with choosing ICSI?

A2: It has been suggested that the risk of congenital anomalies in children born after ICSI may be slightly higher than after natural conception or conventional IVF, but this is thought to include factors arising from male infertility itself. At present there is no firm evidence that ICSI directly causes serious congenital anomalies. It is important to discuss genetic risk fully with your doctor.

### Q3: If fertilization succeeds with ICSI, does pregnancy always follow?

A3: ICSI is a technique that raises the chance of fertilization, but a fertilized egg does not necessarily implant and go on to pregnancy and birth. Many factors influence whether a pregnancy succeeds — embryo quality, the condition of the uterus, age, and more. ICSI is one means of assisting fertilization, and there are many hurdles in the steps that follow.

## Summary

Across 20 randomised trials, ICSI did not improve live birth for non-male-factor infertility, and conventional IVF had a significantly higher clinical pregnancy rate. Set against that, conventional IVF carries a higher risk of total fertilisation failure, and ICSI reduces that risk.

If ICSI is proposed to you, a good question is which of those two things it is meant to address in your case. "To be safe" is not, on its own, an answer the data supports.
