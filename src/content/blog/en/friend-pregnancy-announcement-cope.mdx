Content note: pregnancy, fertility treatment

A friend's pregnancy announcement is joyful news for many people — and at the same time, it is not unusual to feel complicated things about it for all sorts of reasons. For someone who is trying to conceive, or who has experienced a miscarriage, such news can open a deep wound. In this article, I want to start from the premise that your feelings are valid, without denying any of them, and then talk about psychological approaches and practical steps for protecting your own heart without guilt.

## Finding a Friend's Pregnancy Announcement Painful Is a Natural Feeling

When you hear that a friend is pregnant, you may feel guilty for not being able to be wholeheartedly happy. But jealousy, sadness, a sense of loss, the feeling of being left behind, anger — it is entirely natural for many emotions to mix together. These feelings come from your own inner wishes and experiences. What matters is not trying to expel them as "bad," but first recognizing and accepting that you are feeling them. What the English-speaking TTC (trying-to-conceive) community calls "toxic positivity" — the pressure to always stay upbeat, to think positively — actually wears the heart down and leads to suppressed emotion. Whatever your feelings are, they deserve respect.

## Psychological Approaches for Taking Space Without Guilt

To protect your own mental health, temporarily creating some distance in a friendship, or adjusting how you engage, is not a bad thing at all. Here are some psychological approaches.

### Emotional Labeling and Acceptance

Giving your emotions a name and allowing yourself simply to feel them makes those emotions easier to hold. Saying out loud — or simply noticing internally — "Right now I feel jealous about my friend's pregnancy," or "Right now I am sad," softens the sense of being overwhelmed. Emotions are like weather: neither good nor bad, and always moving through.

### Setting Boundaries

Setting healthy boundaries in a friendship is essential to keeping your peace of mind. For example:

*   **Adjust how often you are in contact:** Muting someone's social media posts for a while, or replying to messages less often, is a wise choice made to protect yourself.
*   **Choose where and how you meet:** Skipping large baby showers or gatherings with children, meeting briefly in a calm place instead, or choosing not to meet for a while — all of these deserve respect.
*   **Limit the topics you discuss:** You can let a friend know, directly or simply through how you respond, that you would rather not talk about pregnancy or parenting right now.

### Self-Validation and Self-Compassion

The process of trying to conceive can shake your sense of your own worth. But your worth is not determined by whether you become pregnant. Recognize your efforts and each small thing you accomplish day to day, and practice self-compassion — treating yourself kindly. Rather than blaming yourself, be the friend who stands beside you in a difficult situation.

### Sharing With Trusted Individuals

Holding emotions alone increases the mental load. Telling a partner, family member, trusted friend, or a professional counselor — someone who will listen without dismissing what you feel — can lighten the weight you are carrying. Connecting with people who have the same experience, such as in a TTC community, can also help ease the sense of isolation.

## Caring for Your Mental Health While Trying to Conceive

Trying to conceive can become a long road, and it is unavoidable that you will meet stress and emotional ups and downs along the way. Beyond pregnancy announcements, it matters to listen to your own inner voice in everyday life and to move at a pace that does not strain you. Finding a stress-reduction method that suits you — mindfulness, a hobby, exercise — can help. If you need it, please actively consider support from a mental health professional. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q1: I hate that I cannot say "congratulations." What should I do?**

A: First, please do not blame yourself for being unable to feel joy. Carrying complicated emotions is natural for a human being. Rather than forcing out words you do not feel, sending a brief congratulatory message and then taking some space is one legitimate option. You can create an opportunity to talk face to face later, once you feel ready. Your mental health comes first.

**Q2: I want to take some distance, but I am afraid the friendship will suffer.**

A: In a healthy friendship, your friend will understand and respect your circumstances. Taking distance for a while is not the same as ending a relationship. If you can be honest with each other, you might say, "I'm in a bit of a fragile period right now and would like some quiet time." Adjusting the relationship at your own pace, without straining yourself, is what matters.

**Q3: How much should I tell friends about being in fertility treatment?**

A: This is a very personal decision, and there is no set answer. Talking about it can lighten the load, and it can also add stress. What matters is choosing how much to say, what to say, and whom to say it to. You do not have to share everything — stay within what feels comfortable to you. If it helps, limiting it to a few trusted friends or to a professional is also a valid choice.

## Summary

You are not the only one who feels something complicated at a friend's pregnancy announcement. Not denying those feelings, keeping your sense of your own worth, and setting appropriate boundaries are all very important in protecting your heart. Seeking professional support when you need it is one of the options available to you. Honor your own pace, and put the health of your body and mind first.
