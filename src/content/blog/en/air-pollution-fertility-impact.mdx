Air pollution's impact on general health is well-known, but its specific effects on reproductive function are still being actively researched. For those navigating their [TTC journey](/en/), understanding these potential environmental factors is crucial.

**Please note: The content presented in this article is based on foundational research, such as cell and animal studies, and the effectiveness or impact on human fertility has not yet been definitively established. Further research is ongoing.**

## The Potential Link Between Air Pollution and Fertility

### Associations by pollutant

There is a 2023 meta-analysis of 93,996 Chinese men. It accounted for the timescale over which sperm are produced, taking 0 to 90 days before ejaculation as the exposure window, and examined six pollutants (PMID: 37601219).

*   **PM2.5** — associated with reduced total sperm count (10–14 days before ejaculation) and reduced total motility (10–14 days, 70–90 days, 0–90 days)
*   **PM10** — associated with reduced total sperm count (70–90 days, 0–90 days) and reduced total motility (0–90 days)
*   **NO2 (nitrogen dioxide)** — associated with reduced total sperm count (70–90 days, 0–90 days)
*   **SO2 (sulphur dioxide)** — associated with reduced total motility (0–9 days, 10–14 days, 0–90 days) and reduced total sperm count (0–90 days)

Two things are worth noting. The participants were all Chinese men, so how far this transfers to other regions is unknown. And the exposure windows where associations appeared differ by pollutant rather than lining up consistently.

### How large is the effect?

A separate 2021 meta-analysis gives effect sizes as standardised mean differences (PMID: 34532803).

*   Sperm concentration: **−0.17** (95% CI −0.20, −0.13)
*   Sperm count: **−0.05** (95% CI −0.08, −0.02)
*   Total motility: **−0.33** (95% CI −0.54, −0.11)
*   Progressive motility: **0.00** (95% CI −0.13, 0.12) — **no association**

These are small effects, and progressive motility shows no association at all. That is worth holding in mind against headlines about air pollution and fertility: the direction of the association is consistent for some parameters, but the magnitude is modest, and both analyses pool observational studies that cannot establish causation.

### On the fruit fly study

You may also see basic research cited here. One study reported that substances in tobacco smoke affected sperm quality and motility in *Drosophila melanogaster* (PMID: 41227336). Since tobacco smoke contributes to air pollution, it may offer clues about mechanism.

It does not tell us anything about human fertility. It is an experiment in fruit flies, and the human data above is the relevant evidence for the question this article is about.

### Suspected Mechanisms

Air pollutants are thought to induce oxidative stress and disrupt hormone balance in the body. This could indirectly affect oocyte maturation, fertilization, embryo development, and sperm formation and motility. Some observational studies have hinted at a link between air pollution and male sperm DNA damage or reduced motility, but this causal relationship is not yet established.

These insights underscore the importance of comprehensive [preconception care](/en/blog/preconception-care-today).

### What You Can Do: Reducing Environmental Risks

While it's challenging for individuals to completely avoid air pollution, conscious efforts can help reduce exposure. For example, considering staying indoors on high-pollution days, using air purifiers, and ensuring a smoke-free environment are practical steps. Moreover, maintaining overall health through a balanced diet and adequate sleep is vital for supporting systemic health, including reproductive function, regardless of environmental conditions.

Don't hesitate to seek advice from a specialist. You can find more information and support on our [homepage](/en/).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### Q1: Does air pollution directly cause infertility?

A1: Currently, there isn't strong enough human evidence to definitively state that air pollution is a direct cause of infertility. However, ongoing research suggests the possibility of indirect influences.

### Q2: Can specific pollutants affect reproductive function?

A2: In pooled data from 93,996 men, PM2.5, PM10, NO2 and SO2 were each associated with reduced sperm count or motility (PMID: 37601219). The effect sizes in a separate analysis were small, with no association for progressive motility (PMID: 34532803). Oxidative stress and endocrine disruption are the proposed mechanisms, but these are observational data and do not establish causation.

### Q3: What can I do to mitigate air pollution risks while [TTC](/en/)?

A3: It's recommended to consider reducing outdoor activities on high-pollution days, utilizing air purifiers, and avoiding smoking. Prioritizing general health is also key.

## Summary

The link between air pollution and reproductive function is a developing field of research. While current findings are primarily from cell and animal studies, and direct human impact is not yet clear, the potential for environmental factors to influence health is suggested. We hope this information empowers you to consider your health and future. If you have concerns, don't hesitate to consult with a healthcare professional.
