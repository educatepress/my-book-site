If you are weighing your options in fertility treatment, you have probably heard that acupuncture can help you conceive. Complementary approaches are common alongside conventional treatment, and the information about them varies widely in quality. This article looks at what the evidence actually shows for acupuncture in fertility treatment, and at the particular difficulty of separating an effect from a placebo response.

## Why acupuncture draws interest

Fertility treatment is demanding physically and emotionally, and many couples look for additional ways to improve their chances alongside established care. Acupuncture is among the most widely known of these. In the context of fertility it is often described as reducing stress, improving blood flow, and balancing hormones.

## Proposed mechanisms

Several hypotheses have been proposed for how acupuncture might affect fertility, most of them not fully established.

*   **Blood flow.** It has been suggested that acupuncture improves pelvic blood flow, and with it the ovarian and endometrial environment. Whether that translates into higher pregnancy rates requires further study
*   **Relaxation.** Acupuncture is thought to act on the autonomic nervous system and promote relaxation. Fertility treatment is demanding, and rest matters in itself. That said, **no causal link has been shown between stress and failure to conceive**, and the idea that relaxation raises pregnancy rates has not been established either
*   **Hormonal effects.** Some studies suggest acupuncture may influence sex hormone and stress hormone levels. How that would act on human reproduction, and whether it produces clinically meaningful change, remains unclear

## What the evidence shows

Research on acupuncture in fertility treatment goes back decades. Early studies reported improved pregnancy rates when acupuncture was performed at embryo transfer; larger and more rigorous studies have since produced different results.

**The results depend heavily on what acupuncture was compared against.** Once you see that, the conflicting headlines start to make sense.

**Compared with sham acupuncture.** A single-blind randomized trial at 16 centres in Australia and New Zealand assigned 848 women undergoing IVF to either acupuncture or sham acupuncture, with one treatment during ovarian stimulation and two around embryo transfer. The primary outcome was live birth. **Live births occurred in 74 of 405 women (18.3%) in the acupuncture group and 72 of 404 (17.8%) in the sham group — no difference** (relative risk 1.02, 95% CI 0.76-1.38). The authors state that these findings do not support the use of acupuncture to improve live birth rates among women undergoing IVF (PMID: 29800212).

**Pooling trials with varied comparison groups.** A 2023 meta-analysis of 25 trials totalling 4,757 participants reported a clinical pregnancy rate of 43.6% in acupuncture groups versus 33.2% in controls, and a live birth rate of 38.0% versus 28.7%, both higher with acupuncture (PMID: 37436463). However, the control groups in these trials included **no-treatment groups as well as sham acupuncture**. The authors themselves note that placebo acupuncture is the preferable comparator.

Compared with nothing, a difference appears. Compared with sham needling, it disappears. That pattern indicates the research has not managed to separate the effect of receiving treatment from the effect of the needling itself.

Alongside treatment, looking after your general health and managing stress remain worthwhile. For more on that, see [preconception care](/en/blog/preconception-care-today).

## The limits and the value of placebo effects

A placebo effect is a real change in how someone feels following a treatment with no pharmacological action, driven by the expectation of benefit. In acupuncture research, controlling for it is unusually difficult.

Trials typically compare real needling with sham needling designed not to penetrate the skin. Keeping participants unaware of which they received is hard, and expectation and other psychological factors can influence reported outcomes.

That placebo effects contribute to wellbeing is not in doubt. Feeling cared for, feeling less pain — these improve quality of life. Whether they alter reproductive function is a separate question, and current practice in fertility treatment prioritises interventions with demonstrated effects.

## If you are considering acupuncture

*   **Tell your fertility doctor.** Share what you are doing so it can be considered alongside your treatment plan
*   **Treat it as complementary.** It does not replace established fertility treatment
*   **Choose a qualified practitioner.** Check credentials, hygiene standards, and whether you receive a proper explanation
*   **Consider time and cost.** It is usually not covered by insurance. Keep it within what is sustainable for you

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Q1: Can acupuncture alone treat infertility?
A1: It is not a substitute for established fertility treatment. It is generally considered a complementary option.

### Q2: Who might find it useful?
A2: People seeking relaxation or relief from the strain of treatment may find value in it. Whether it fits your situation is worth discussing with your doctor.

### Q3: What should I watch for?
A3: Choose a qualified practitioner working in clean conditions, and make sure your fertility doctor knows you are receiving acupuncture.

## Summary

In the largest sham-controlled randomized trial to date (848 women), acupuncture produced no difference in live birth rates. Meta-analyses that pool trials with varied comparison groups do show a difference, but whether that difference comes from the needling itself or simply from receiving treatment has not been separated out.

None of this means acupuncture is worthless. Reduced stress and improved wellbeing are real benefits for some people. If you are considering it, treat it as a complement to standard care, work with practitioners you trust, and judge it calmly. Whatever you decide, your feelings about this process deserve respect.
